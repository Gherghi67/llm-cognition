The paper
The role of System 1 and System 2 semantic memory structure in human and LLM biases
Katherine Abramski, Giulio Rossetti, Massimo Stella · arXiv 2604.12816 · April 2026

What the paper builds

For humans, Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, the authors build one word network with three layers over the same ~12,000 cue words. Free associations stand for fast, intuitive System 1 memory. Definitions and category relations stand for slow, deliberate System 2 memory. Then they ask two things: can the layers be merged without losing structure, and how much gender bias does each layer carry?

~12k cue words 3M+ SWOW responses 3 networks × 3 layers 10 gender primes 172 targets 4 topics 1,720 prime–target pairs

The method, in order

Two parts. Part A builds the networks and tests whether their layers can merge. Part B measures gender bias in each layer.

Part A · Build the networks

Table 2 · Figure 3
  1. 1

    Collect three kinds of word data for the same cue words

    LayerHumansLLMs
    Free associations SWOW (Small World of Words): ~12k cues, 3M+ responses from thousands of people LWOW: the same cues given to each model, following the SWOW procedure
    Definitions WordNet definitions The model lists each word's senses, then defines each sense
    Categories WordNet synonyms, antonyms, hyponyms, hypernyms The model gives the same four relations for each sense
  2. 2

    Turn each into a network

    Associations: cue linked to each response, weighted by how often people (or the model) gave it. Made undirected, keeping the larger weight when both directions exist. Definitions: cue linked to every content word in its definitions, so doctor links to person, licensed, practice, medicine. Categories: cue linked to each related word. Definitions and categories are unweighted.

  3. 3

    Clean up

    Drop words that aren't in WordNet. In the associations layer, drop links given only once (weight 1). Keep only the largest connected component. The result is Table 2.

  4. 4

    Test whether the layers can merge

    The Python package multired (De Domenico et al., 2015) represents each layer by its normalized Laplacian. It repeatedly merges the pair of layers that loses the least structural information and computes the relative entropy after each merge. The configuration with the highest relative entropy is the best description. That curve is Figure 3.

Part A result

Humans are best described with all three layers kept separate. Mistral and Llama 3 are best described with associations and categories merged.

Part B · Measure gender bias

Table 1 · Figures 2, 4, 5 · Appendix 6–14
  1. 5

    Pick primes and targets

    10 primes: woman, female, mother, girl, feminine and man, male, father, boy, masculine. 172 targets in four topics (traits, home vs career, art vs science, professions), half female-stereotyped and half male-stereotyped (Table 1). That gives 1,720 prime–target pairs.

  2. 6

    Spread activation from each prime

    With the R package spreadr (Siew, 2019), activation starts on a prime and flows through one layer. The activation that reaches a target stands in for how quickly a person would link the two words. Settings come from the authors' earlier work:

    start activation = number of nodes steps = 2 × diameter retention = 0.5
  3. 7

    Score the bias

    Normalize final activations to control for word frequency. Compare stereotype-consistent pairs (woman–nurse, man–doctor) with inconsistent ones (woman–doctor, man–nurse). The differences become standardized effect sizes using a paired Wilcoxon test. Positive means stereotype-consistent bias. Figure 2 illustrates the idea.

  4. 8

    Aggregate

    Per topic, layer and gender gives Figure 4. Across all four topics gives Figure 5. The normalized activations themselves, prime by target, are the appendix heatmaps (Figures 6–14).

Part B result

In humans, bias is consistently lower in the two System 2 layers, as the authors predicted. Mistral and Llama 3 show no consistent pattern across topics, and Llama 3 links all professions more strongly to men.

Questions to expect

Where the methods are thin. When the paper doesn't say, say that it doesn't.

  1. 1. Is WordNet really “human” System 2 memory?
    No people produced it. It's a curated dictionary. The LLM side is the models asked to reproduce WordNet-style content, so those layers compare a reference resource with the models' recall of it.
  2. 2. What prompts and settings made the definitions and categories?
    The paper describes two steps (list the senses, then define or relate each one) but gives no prompt text, temperature or sample count. Its Limitations section says results may partly depend on how the prompts were worded.
  3. 3. What exactly is the “effect size” in Figures 4 and 5?
    Differences between consistent and inconsistent pairs, turned into standardized effect sizes with a paired Wilcoxon test. The paper doesn't name the statistic or say what is paired, by target or by prime.
  4. 4. Is the drop in bias for System 2 significant?
    No p-values, confidence intervals or interaction tests are reported. The claim rests on the pattern in the figures.
  5. 5. How solid is the reducibility result?
    One run per network, with no relative-entropy values in the text and no bootstrap. The layers also have different node counts (humans: 24,308 / 16,200 / 34,211), and the paper doesn't explain how nodes are matched across layers.
  6. 6. Could the filtering change the results?
    Possibly. Dropping non-WordNet words removes some legitimate LLM output, and only the largest component is kept. No sensitivity check is reported.
  7. 7. Smaller details
    “Assertive” was missing from the networks, so “assert” was used. LWOW also covers Haiku, which isn't analysed here. Models are named only as mistral-7b and llama3.1-8b, with no exact checkpoint.
